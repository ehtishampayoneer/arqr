How UK Shoe Stores Cut Look and Colour Returns with AR (Augmented Reality)

Picture a shoe store owner in Manchester. She sells leather shoes and boots from one shop and a website that ships across the UK. Her returns shelf tells a story she knows well. Plenty of pairs come back, and not always because the size was wrong. The colour read warmer on screen than it does at home. The boot looked slimmer in the photo than it does on a real foot. The shoe did not work with the coat and jeans the shopper had in mind.

Those are look returns. They cost the same as any other return. Postage both ways, a pair that goes back on the shelf, staff time spent on the exchange, and a shopper who may not come back. AR (Augmented Reality) takes aim at exactly this kind of return, because it shows the shoe on the shopper's own feet, in their own light, before they buy.

Why good photos still mislead

A product photo is honest work, and it still has limits. Studio light is not hallway light. A photo taken from one flattering angle hides the bulk of a boot. A colour that looks rich against a white background can read flat next to a navy coat. The shopper is not careless. They are guessing, because a flat photo cannot show them the shoe in their own world.

That guess is where look returns are born. The shopper orders with hope, opens the box with a picture in mind, and the real shoe does not match it. Nothing was wrong with the shoe. The picture in their head was wrong.

What a true to size view shows that a photo cannot

With AR (Augmented Reality), the shopper points their phone at their own feet and sees the shoe at its true size, in the light of their own room. Three things change at once.

First, colour. The shopper sees the tan or the black against their own floor and their own clothes, not against a studio background. Second, bulk. A chunky boot looks chunky on a real foot, and a slim shoe looks slim. There is no flattering angle to hide behind. Third, the wardrobe test. The shopper can hold the phone up and ask the only question that matters: does this shoe work with what I already own?

That is the whole mechanism. Fewer surprises at the door means fewer pairs going back in the post.

The returns maths, in plain terms

Picture a shop that takes back 12 pairs a month because the look was wrong. Say a true to size view on the shopper's own feet would have changed the mind of 4 of those shoppers before they ordered. That is 4 fewer parcels going out and coming back, 4 fewer pairs to recheck and reshelf, and 4 shoppers who kept a pair they actually wanted.

Run it with your own numbers. Count your look returns for one month, not your total returns. Look returns are the ones your AR (Augmented Reality) views can reach. Then ask what 4 fewer returns a month, or 2, or 6, would be worth in postage, staff time, and kept sales. The maths is yours. The shape of it is simple: every return that never happens is money the shop keeps.

One number worth knowing, and it is Shopify's own data, not ours. Shopify reports that products with 3D or AR views convert about 94% better than products without. You do not need anywhere near that figure for the maths to work. A handful of saved sales and a handful of avoided returns cover a small store's AR cost many times over.

One honest limit to keep in mind

Be plain about what AR (Augmented Reality) does for shoes and what it does not. It shows the true size and the look. It lets a shopper compare a shoe against their own feet and their own wardrobe. It cannot tell a shopper whether the shoe feels comfortable. No view can measure comfort, and any provider who says otherwise is selling you something.

That limit does not weaken the case. Plenty of shoe returns are look returns. The colour is off, the style is bulkier than expected, the shoe does not work with the wardrobe.
