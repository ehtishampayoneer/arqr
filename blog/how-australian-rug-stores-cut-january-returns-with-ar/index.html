How Australian Rug Stores Cut January Returns with AR (Augmented Reality)

Picture a rug store owner in Sydney. She sells handmade rugs from one shop and a website that ships across the country. December is her biggest month. Then January arrives, and the boxes start coming back.

January is the month the rugs come back. A rug bought as a Christmas gift that does not suit the room it was meant for. A rug bought in the Boxing Day sale, in a rush, in the wrong size for the open plan living room. A rug that looked warm in the shop and looks different in the bright summer light at home. The customer is not unhappy with the rug itself. She just could not tell how it would look in her own room before she bought it.

Every return costs the store twice. The sale is lost, and the store pays the freight both ways, the repacking, and the time to put the rug back on the shelf. A large rug ships at real money, and a heavy January can wipe out the whole December profit. The fix is not a stricter returns policy. The fix is to let the shopper check the size and the colour before the order is placed.

Why January returns hit Australian rug stores hardest

Most of the year, the shopper buys for herself. She knows her room, she knows her sofa, and she has time to think. In December, that changes. She buys for someone else, in a hurry, for a room she has only seen once or twice. She guesses the size from a photo and hopes for the best.

Rugs are the worst category for guessing. The size is listed in centimetres, but almost nobody measures. The buyer looks at a photo, feels the size, and hopes. A rug that looks right on a screen can swallow a room or get lost in it. Colour is the same problem. A rug photographed in studio light can look warmer or cooler than it does on the floor at home, and Australian summer light is strong and honest.

That is why the returns land in January and not in March. The gift season packs a whole quarter of guessing into four weeks, and the bill for it arrives the week after the holidays. Add the Boxing Day sale, where shoppers buy fast and think later, and January gets heavier still.

The real cause is a size question, not a quality question

When a rug store reads its January returns, the pattern is plain. Very few rugs come back damaged or faulty. Most come back with a note that says some version of the same thing: too big, too small, does not fit the room, wrong colour in our light.

Those are all questions the shopper asked herself before buying, and answered by guessing. She measured nothing. She held nothing up to her floor. She trusted a flat photo and a size in the description, and the photo could not show her the truth.

An AR (Augmented Reality) view answers the question properly. The shopper points her phone at her own floor, and the rug appears there at its true size, in the light of her own room. She can see if the rug suits the sofa or crowds it. She can see if the pattern works with her floorboards or fights them. She buys once she knows, and she keeps what she buys.

What changes when the shopper checks first

Three things change for the store, and they all show up in January.

First, wrong size returns fall. The shopper who checked the rug against her own floor does not buy the wrong size. She buys the right one the first time, or she picks a different size that fits and keeps that one instead.

Second, wrong colour returns fall. Colour and scale are the two things a flat photo hides best. A shopper who saw the rug in her own room, in her own light, already knows how it looks there. There is no surprise when the box opens.

Third, the store stops paying for the guessing. Every return that never happens is freight the store never pays and shelf time the store never spends. For a small store shipping large rugs across the country, that saving alone is worth more than the cost of the AR itself.

One more number, and it is Shopify's own data, not ours: products with 3D or AR views convert about 94% better than products without. Shoppers who can check the true size hesitate less, and rug shoppers hesitate most of all, because the whole question is how the rug looks in their room.
