How New Zealand Decor Stores Cut January Returns with AR (Augmented Reality)

Picture a home decor store owner in Christchurch. She sells wall art, lamps, vases, and mirrors, from one shop and a website that ships across both islands. December is her biggest month. Then January arrives, and the boxes start coming back.

January is the month the decor comes back. A vase bought as a gift that does not suit the room. Wall art in the wrong size for the wall it was meant for. A lamp bought in a rush that clashes with the sofa once it is home. The customer is not unhappy with the piece itself. She just could not tell how it would look in her own room before she bought it.

Every return costs the store twice. The sale is lost, and the store pays the freight, the repacking, and the time to put the piece back on the shelf. For a small New Zealand decor store, a heavy January can wipe out the whole Christmas profit. The fix is not a stricter returns policy. The fix is to let the shopper check the size and the look before the order is placed.

Why January returns hit decor stores hardest

Most of the year, the shopper buys for herself. She knows her room, she knows her sofa, and she has time to think. In December, that changes. She buys for someone else, in a hurry, for a room she has only seen once or twice. She guesses the size from a photo and hopes for the best.

Decor is the worst category for guessing. A rug has a size in centimetres that a shopper can measure against her floor. A vase or a wall art piece is judged by eye, and the eye lies. A lamp that looks tall in a product photo can look small next to a real sofa. A mirror that looks right on a white studio wall can look wrong on a warm cream wall at home.

That is why the returns land in January and not in March. The gift season packs a whole quarter of guessing into four weeks, and the bill for it arrives the week after Christmas.

The real cause is a size question, not a quality question

When a decor store reads its January returns, the pattern is plain. Very few pieces come back broken or faulty. Most come back with a note that says some version of the same thing: too big, too small, does not suit the room, wrong colour in our light.

Those are all questions the shopper asked herself before buying, and answered by guessing. She measured nothing. She held nothing up to her wall. She trusted a flat photo on a white background and a size in the description, and the photo could not show her the truth.

An AR (Augmented Reality) view answers the question properly. The shopper points her phone at her own wall or her own side table, and the piece appears there at its true size, in the light of her own room. She can see if the wall art fills the space or gets lost in it. She can see if the lamp stands taller than the sofa arm or shorter. She buys once she knows, and she keeps what she buys.

What changes when the shopper checks first

Three things change for the store, and they all show up in January.

First, wrong size returns fall. The shopper who checked the piece against her own wall does not buy the wrong size. She buys the right one the first time, or she picks a different piece that fits and keeps that one instead.

Second, does not suit the room returns fall. Colour and scale are the two things a flat photo hides best. A shopper who saw the piece in her own room, in her own light, already knows how it looks there. There is no surprise when the box opens.

Third, the store stops paying for the guessing. Every return that never happens is freight the store never pays and shelf time the store never spends. For a small store shipping across the country, that saving alone is worth more than the cost of the AR itself.

One more number, and it is Shopify's own data, not ours: products with 3D or AR views convert about 94% better than products without. Shoppers who can check the true size hesitate less, and decor shoppers hesitate most of all, because the whole question is how the piece looks in their room.
