How New Zealand Home Decor Stores Cut Returns with AR (Augmented Reality)

Picture a home decor store owner in Wellington. She sells vases, lamps, mirrors, and wall art from one shop and a website. A fair share of her online orders come back. The reason is almost always the same. The piece looked right on screen and wrong in the room. Too tall for the side table. Too wide for the wall. The colour worked, the size did not.

That is the decor returns problem in one line. It is not quality, and it is not taste. It is size, guessed from a photo.

Why decor returns are mostly size returns

Decor is bought for a spot. A lamp is bought for a table of a known size. A mirror is bought for a wall of a known width. A vase is bought for a shelf with a set height. The shopper knows the spot. What they cannot tell from a photo is whether the piece fits it.

A tape measure helps, but few shoppers measure twice, and fewer still can picture the result from numbers alone. So they order, they hope, and when the piece lands they send it back. The return costs the store the postage both ways, the time to check and re-list the piece, and often the sale itself, because the shopper has already moved on.

Picture a shopper in Christchurch who orders a round wall mirror for the wall above her console table. The photo showed it above a similar table, and it looked about right. In her room it hangs wider than the table and crowds the hallway. She packs it up the same day. Nothing was wrong with the mirror. The size was a guess, and the guess was wrong.

What changes when the shopper sees it in the room

AR (Augmented Reality) puts the piece in the shopper's own room at its true size before they buy. They point the phone at the console table and the mirror appears above it, the right width, the right height, in the light of their own room. They step back. They check it against the wall. The guesswork is gone, and with it goes most of the reason to send the piece back.

That is the whole mechanism. The shopper stops imagining the size and starts checking it. A piece that fits the spot gets ordered with confidence. A piece that does not fit never gets ordered, which is a sale you lose cleanly instead of a return you pay for twice.

Where the view has to live

A true-to-size view only cuts returns if shoppers actually use it, and they use it where the buying decision happens. Three spots matter.

  • Next to the price on every product page. That is where size doubt lives. A see-it-at-true-size link beside the buy button answers the question at the moment it matters.
  • On the price tags in the shop. A QR code on the tag opens the same piece in AR. Customers check it at home and come back decided, instead of buying two sizes and returning one.
  • In Instagram posts and stories. The link opens on a phone, which is exactly where your shoppers browse. A follower who can check the size in their own room is a follower who can order without a trip to the shop.
