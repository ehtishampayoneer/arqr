How Australian Shoe Stores Sell Summer Stock with True-to-Size AR Try-On

Picture a shoe store owner in Melbourne. Her summer range lands in November: white sneakers, leather sandals, canvas slip-ons. Half her sales now come from her website, and sizes vary across brands. A 39 in one brand is a 40 in another. Her customers guess. The guesses become returns, and the returns eat her margin.

This is the honest version.

Summer shoes are the hardest sell on a screen

Winter boots are forgiving. Thick socks, roomy soles, a little space. Summer shoes are not. A sandal that is half a size off rubs. A sneaker that is half a size off blisters. And sizing is the least standardized thing in footwear. One brand's 39 measures 24.5 cm, another's 24.9. The size guide on the product page lists one set of numbers. The customer's feet do not read size guides.

So shoppers do the rational thing. They order two sizes and return one. Some stores call this bracketing. Whatever you call it, you pay the shipping both ways, and you inspect and restock a shoe someone has already walked in.

What true-to-size AR try-on changes

AR try-on answers the one question the photo cannot: how big is this shoe against my own foot. The customer opens the link on their phone, points it at their feet, and sees the shoe at its true size on their own feet, in the light of their own room.

It does not promise fit. Fit is comfort, and no screen can feel comfort. That is the honest line, and we keep it. What AR shows is size and look: the shoe at its real dimensions, compared against your own feet, from the angles you actually see. For summer shoes, where the wrong size rubs on day one, that is the question that decides the sale.
