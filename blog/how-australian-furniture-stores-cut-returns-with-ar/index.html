How Australian Furniture Stores Cut Returns with AR (No App Needed)

Picture a furniture store owner in Brisbane. She sells sofas and dining tables from one showroom and a website that ships across Queensland. A customer in Townsville buys a three-seater from her product page. It arrives, and it swallows the room. Back it comes. She pays the freight both ways and restocks a sofa she cannot resell as new. That story repeats a few times a month.

This is the plain-English version of why that happens and what actually fixes it.

Why sofas come back

Most furniture returns are not quality problems. They are size problems. The customer read the dimensions, looked at the photos, and guessed. Photos lie about scale. A 220cm sofa looks the same size as a 180cm one in a square product photo. The numbers are on the page, but numbers on a screen do not land in a room. People cannot feel 40cm of extra width from a spec table.

In Australia the cost of that guess is brutal. A returned sofa can travel hundreds or thousands of kilometres. Freight eats the margin twice, once there and once back. And a big share of online buyers live nowhere near your showroom. They cannot come and sit on it first. The photo and the spec table are all they get. That is the gap your product page has to close.

What changes when the customer can check the fit first

Now picture the same customer in Townsville. She taps a link on the product page. Her phone camera opens. She points it at her floor and the three-seater appears in her living room, at its true size. She walks around it. She sees whether it clears the doorway and whether it eats the rug. She buys knowing, or she does not buy at all. Either way, the sofa does not come back over a surprise.

This is what true-to-size AR (Augmented Reality) does. It answers the one question photos cannot: will it fit my room. It opens in the phone browser from a link or a QR code. The customer installs nothing. That matters more than it sounds. Most people will not download an app to look at a sofa.

Put the link next to the price on every product page, where the buying decision happens. A link buried in a menu nobody opens does nothing. In the shop, the QR code goes on the floor tags: a browsing customer scans the sofa they are standing in front of, checks it in their own room later that night, and comes back decided. That is the whole system.
